	- ### Origins of Incest Taboos
	  **Theories:**
	- 1. **Biological/Evolutionary:**
		- Inbreeding avoidance hypothesis: Reduces risk of genetic disorders (Westermarck effect - decreased sexual attraction between co-reared individuals).
		- Observed in primate societies and early human groups.
	- 2. **Social/Structural:**
		- Alliance theory (Lévi-Strauss): Encourages exogamy to create inter-group alliances.
		- Maintains family structure by preventing role confusion (parent-child/sibling sexual competition).
	- 3. **Cultural:**
		- Emerged with kinship systems (~50,000 years ago) as symbolic culture developed.
		- Varies cross-culturally (e.g., royal families like Ptolemaic Egypt permitted sibling marriage).
	- **Historical Evidence:**
		- Earliest legal prohibitions appear in 2nd millennium BCE (Code of Hammurabi, Leviticus 18).
		- Universality debated: Some cultures practiced strategic consanguinity despite general taboos.

	- The origin of incest taboos is complex, involving both genetic and cultural factors.  Genetically, inbreeding increases the risk of genetic disorders.  The Westermarck effect, a decreased sexual attraction between individuals raised together, may also play a role in reducing inbreeding.  Early human groups likely avoided inbreeding due to the observed negative consequences.  
	  
	  Culturally, the taboo developed alongside kinship systems approximately 50,000 years ago, as symbolic culture evolved.  Theories suggest that exogamy (marrying outside one's group) fostered inter-group alliances and prevented role confusion within families.  However, the universality of the incest taboo is debated.  Some cultures, like the Ptolemaic Egyptians, practiced sibling marriage despite broader societal taboos.  The earliest legal prohibitions against incest emerged around the 2nd millennium BCE, as seen in the Code of Hammurabi and Leviticus 18.  The balance between genetic and cultural influences in shaping the development of incest taboos is still under discussion.
